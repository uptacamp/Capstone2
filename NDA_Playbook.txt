NDA PLAYBOOK

Guidelines for Reviewing and Signing Third-Party Paper Non-Disclosure
Agreements

Sample Template — For Illustrative Purposes Only | Version 1.0

1. Purpose & Scope

This playbook governs how employees and Legal reviewers evaluate a
Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA) drafted on a counterparty's
(“third-party”) template, rather than our own standard form. It applies
to mutual and one-way NDAs entered into for business discussions, vendor
evaluations, RFPs, M&A diligence, and similar purposes.

The default position is to use our own NDA template. Third-party paper
should only be accepted where our template is rejected by the
counterparty and the terms below are met.

2. General Principles

-   Template-first: Always attempt to lead with our own template first;
    only fall back to third-party paper when necessary to keep the
    business relationship moving.

-   Match the disclosure direction: Confirm whether disclosure will flow
    one-way or both ways. A one-way NDA where we are the only discloser
    needs stricter protections than a mutual NDA.

-   Track everything: Every NDA reviewed under this playbook must be
    logged in the contract management system with counterparty name,
    effective date, term, and reviewer.

-   Escalate on doubt: This playbook does not override specific
    instructions from Legal on a given deal; when in doubt, escalate
    rather than guess.

3. Term Classification (Green / Yellow / Red)

Every clause in a third-party NDA should be classified using the
traffic-light system below before it is routed for approval.

4. Rules for Acceptable Terms (Green Flags)

Rule 1: Confidentiality term of 2–3 years post-termination, or 3–5 years
for trade secrets.

  Matches common market practice and our own template; no special
  approval needed.

Rule 2: Standard carve-outs for information that is public,
independently developed, rightfully known before disclosure, or
rightfully received from a third party.

  These are baseline exclusions found in essentially every enforceable
  NDA.

Rule 3: Mutual obligations that are symmetric — both parties owe the
same duties regardless of who discloses more.

  Symmetry avoids one-sided risk and rarely needs negotiation.

Rule 4: Governing law and venue in a neutral, well-established
commercial jurisdiction (e.g., Delaware, New York, England & Wales).

  Predictable forums reduce litigation risk and cost if a dispute
  arises.

Rule 5: A standard “residuals” clause is absent, or if present, limited
to unaided memory of general skills/knowledge.

  Aligns with our own position and avoids diluting protection over
  disclosed materials.

5. Rules for Terms Requiring Negotiation (Yellow Flags)

Rule 6: Term is longer than 5 years but not perpetual, or automatically
renews without notice.

  Fallback: negotiate down to 3–5 years, or add a right to terminate on
  30 days' notice.

Rule 7: Non-solicitation or non-compete language bundled into the NDA.

  Fallback: strike entirely, or narrow to solicitation of employees
  directly staffed on the engagement for 12 months.

Rule 8: Broad, one-sided indemnification for breach of confidentiality
obligations.

  Fallback: make indemnification mutual, or cap at direct damages
  arising from a party's own breach.

Rule 9: Assignment permitted freely by counterparty but restricted for
us, or silent on assignment in an M&A context.

  Fallback: make assignment rights symmetric, with consent required
  except for assignment to a successor via merger or sale of
  substantially all assets.

Rule 10: Definition of “Confidential Information” omits a marking or
reasonable-identification requirement for orally disclosed information.

  Fallback: add a requirement that oral disclosures be identified as
  confidential at the time, or summarized in writing within 15–30 days.

Rule 11: Injunctive relief clause that also waives our right to require
a bond.

  Fallback: keep injunctive relief available but remove the bond waiver,
  or make it mutual.

6. Rules for Unacceptable Terms (Red Flags — Must Reject or Escalate)

Rule 12: Perpetual confidentiality obligations with no defined end date.

  Creates open-ended liability that cannot be reasonably tracked or
  insured against; must be time-bound.

Rule 13: Automatic, irrevocable license or assignment of intellectual
property, feedback, or improvements disclosed under the NDA.

  An NDA should protect information, not transfer ownership of IP; this
  belongs in a separate, deliberately negotiated agreement, if at all.

Rule 14: Exclusivity or standstill provisions restricting who we may
talk to, invest in, or compete with.

  These are business-strategic commitments that go far beyond
  confidentiality and require executive/board-level approval, not
  routine NDA review.

Rule 15: One-sided limitation of liability that fully exempts the
counterparty while leaving us fully exposed.

  Creates asymmetric risk; liability treatment should be mutual or
  removed from the NDA entirely.

Rule 16: Governing law in a jurisdiction with unpredictable enforcement
of confidentiality or IP rights, or mandatory arbitration seated there.

  Increases enforcement risk disproportionately; requires Senior Counsel
  sign-off if it cannot be renegotiated.

Rule 17: Publicity or announcement rights permitting the counterparty to
name us or reference the relationship without our consent.

  Unauthorized public association can create reputational, securities,
  or customer-relations risk.

Rule 18: Audit rights allowing the counterparty to inspect our systems,
facilities, or records without reasonable notice, scope limits, or
confidentiality protections of their own.

  Unbounded audit rights create security and operational exposure
  disproportionate to a confidentiality agreement.

Rule 19: Automatic “deemed acceptance” clauses that bind us to
amendments the counterparty posts or sends without our signature.

  Removes our ability to consent to material changes in terms over time.

8. Signature Authority

-   Business owners may sign NDAs classified entirely Green under
    Section 4, provided the counterparty and purpose are properly
    logged.

-   Contract Managers may sign NDAs with only Yellow deviations that
    have been resolved using the approved fallback language in Section
    5.

-   In-house Counsel or a Senior Counsel/GC designee must sign, or
    expressly approve signature of, any NDA containing a Red flag under
    Section 6.

-   No employee may sign an NDA on behalf of the company outside their
    delegated authority, regardless of how routine the document appears.

9. Notes

This is a sample/illustrative playbook intended to show the structure
and type of rules a real NDA playbook might contain. It does not reflect
any specific company's actual legal positions, risk tolerance, or
approved fallback language, and should not be relied upon as legal
advice.
